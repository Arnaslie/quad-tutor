# Domain v2: Single-Campus, Course-Scoped, Peer-Tutored (UA beachhead)

**Author:** DOMAIN teammate · **Date:** 2026-09-18 · Supersedes strategy in `01-domain-betterhelp.md` (left intact as history)
**Audience:** founder + ARCHITECTURE + UI/UX

**Headline:** The decisions are good ones and the recommendation survives — but the single most important fact I found in this round is that **the closest possible precedent, Knack, started as exactly this business (B2C, peer tutors, course-matched, launched out of a university incubator), got onto 100+ campuses, and then moved the payer from the student to the institution.** That is not a reason to stop. It is a reason to know, on day one, what you would do if student-pays turns out to be the weaker payer — and to build so that pivot costs you a pricing page, not a rewrite.

Second headline: **campus-by-campus is a replication business, not a network-effects business.** Campus #2 does not get easier because campus #1 worked. Plan accordingly — it changes what "traction" means.

Confidence ledger at the end. Same standard as round 1.

## 1. Pressure-testing the single-campus beachhead

### 1.1 What the precedents actually say

**Knack — the direct precedent, and the most valuable thing in this document. [verified]**
- Founded at the University of Florida's "Gator Hatchery" incubator, Spring 2016; won UF's business plan competition ($25k) which seeded its first raise.
- **"In its earliest days, before partnering directly with institutions, Knack began as a direct-to-consumer tutoring platform where students would pay out of pocket."**
- Grew "grassroots, guerrilla marketing" onto **100+ campuses**.
- The pivot trigger: *a student government leader at a big state school reached out wanting to subsidize Knack* because they felt the campus wasn't reaching enough students. Knack realized the payer could be the institution.
- COVID 10x'd revenue and proved out the B2B model. **Knack no longer actively markets its D2C model.**
- Today: institution-funded, free to students at partner schools. Public deal size: **$540K with the University of Florida**. Partners include U Michigan-Dearborn, USF College of Nursing, New College of Florida, Fort Valley State. Raised a Series B (New Markets Venture Partners, 2025), new CEO 2025.
- Their pitch line is almost word-for-word the founder's wedge: tutors "who have previously aced the exact courses they are offering assistance in."

Read this carefully, because it cuts three ways:
1. **Validation.** Course-scoped peer tutoring on campus is a real, fundable, still-growing business. Someone raised a Series B on it.
2. **Warning.** The team that knew this market best concluded the *student* was the weaker payer and moved to the *institution*. Nobody has demonstrated that student-pays works at scale in this exact niche.
3. **Opening.** Knack now sells to procurement offices. That is a 6–18 month enterprise sales cycle **[assumption on cycle length]** and it means Knack is structurally absent from any campus that hasn't signed. There are thousands of those. A D2C product can be live at UA next month; Knack cannot be unless UA buys it.

**Yik Yak — the campus-bubble cautionary tale. [verified]** Explosive campus PMF, tens of millions raised, then collapse. The specific failure modes that map onto this product: *"users were mostly college kids sharing in esoteric campus bubbles, Yik Yak fell apart when they left for summer or graduated"* and *"don't expand too quickly — moderators needed on each new campus."* Both are literally your §5 and §6 problems.

**StudySoup / NoteShark / Flashnotes — the campus-marketplace graveyard. [verified]** StudySoup raised $1.7M, claimed 2,000 notetakers across 150 schools, and quietly stopped being a marketplace. NoteShark shut down Dec 2016 and named its own cause: *"not actively communicating legality to Northwestern faculty; professors didn't want classroom notes traded in a marketplace."* FSU publicly disavowed StudySoup and objected to its use of the university's logo. Flashnotes shut down May 2024.

**Tutorpeers — a peer-tutoring marketplace that goes offline June 1, 2026. [verified]** Stated reason is the generic one: growth, funding and market conditions didn't align.

**Facebook/Tinder/Bumble** are the campus-expansion successes everyone cites, and they are **the wrong analogy** — they crossed campuses because students had friends at other campuses. Tutoring has essentially zero cross-campus network effect.

### 1.2 What makes campus-by-campus work, and what makes it fail

**Works when:**
- The campus is a genuine *dense, closed liquidity pool*. Everyone in MATH 125 is within a 15-minute walk, shares a syllabus and an exam date, and knows someone who took it last year. That density is why the cold start is tractable at all — you don't need a national marketplace, you need ~10 tutors in ~5 courses.
- Distribution is physical and social, not paid. Greek life, dorm floors, majors' clubs, GroupMe course chats, the line outside the free tutoring center the night before an exam, professors and TAs who quietly refer. **This is the only affordable channel and it is the entire go-to-market.**
- Word of mouth is dense and fast. In a single course section, a good session gets talked about in the GroupMe within hours.
- The founder has a real insider — the advisor's UA connection is the actual asset here. Treat it as such.

**Fails when:**
- You expand before one campus is genuinely profitable, and end up with 20 half-liquid campuses (the StudySoup pattern: impressive school count, no depth).
- The university turns hostile. NoteShark and StudySoup both got burned on faculty and administration relations. **Do not use UA marks, logos, or the name in a way that implies affiliation.** Assume the university may, at some point, notice you.
- Demand is seasonal and the bubble empties (Yik Yak).
- You mistake course-level liquidity for campus-level liquidity. 40,000 students is irrelevant; what matters is: are there 3 available tutors for CH 101 on Thursday night before the exam? Liquidity is per-course-per-week, and it is much thinner than the headline suggests.

### 1.3 Crossing to campus #2 and #3 — does it reset?

**Honest answer: demand resets to zero; supply and ops partially transfer; product and playbook fully transfer.**

What transfers to Auburn or Tennessee:
- The software, the matching logic, the payments, the operational runbook.
- The recruiting script and the knowledge of *what kind* of student makes a reliable tutor.
- Credibility with investors, and possibly with tutors ("we already run this at UA").
- A named-course launch methodology (§9).

What does **not** transfer:
- **The course catalog.** MATH 125 at UA is meaningless at Auburn. The entire matching asset — courses, sections, professors, exam patterns, the accumulated per-course knowledge — is campus-local and rebuilt from scratch.
- Brand and trust among students. Zero.
- Supply. Zero. Every tutor must be recruited locally.
- Social distribution. You need new insiders in new Greek houses and new GroupMes.

**Therefore campus #2 is roughly as hard as campus #1, minus the product build and plus a playbook.** Call it 50–70% of the effort **[assumption — a judgment, not a measured figure]**. It is franchising, not networking.

The strategic consequences are sharp and the founder should internalize them now:
1. **A single campus must be profitable standalone.** There is no "we'll make it up at scale" — scale here is just more copies of the same unit. If UA doesn't work economically, 20 campuses don't fix it; they multiply it.
2. **The scaling constraint is launch labor, not software.** The thing to build and refine at UA is a *repeatable campus launch process*, and to measure how many weeks of one person's time a campus takes. That number is the business model.
3. **Don't launch campus #2 until UA has a full, profitable fall-to-spring cycle.** Otherwise you are optimizing a playbook you haven't validated.
4. **Pick campus #2 for adjacency, not size.** Same state or conference, similar course structure, and — critically — somewhere you have another insider. Auburn, UAB, Mississippi State, Tennessee. **[assumption: adjacency reduces launch cost; unproven.]**
5. Watch for the one genuine cross-campus asset: **graduating tutors.** A UA tutor who goes to grad school elsewhere is a warm supply-side seed. Thin, but real, and it's the only network effect available.

## 2. Peer-supply economics: what student tutors need to be paid

### 2.1 The wage floor at UA [verified]
- **General on-campus student jobs: ~$10/hour minimum.**
- **America Reads and Counts tutoring program (UA Student Employment Services): $12/hour.**
- **UA Athletics tutor position: $10.00 minimum, $17.50 midpoint**, varying by subject and teaching experience.
- **[unverified]** I could not find a published rate specifically for the Center for Academic Success / Learning Commons peer tutors. **This is the single most important number for the founder to get from the UA contact.** Ask directly: what does CAS pay its peer tutors per hour, and how many hours do they get?

So the institutional wage floor is roughly **$10–17.50/hour**, and the most likely CAS peer-tutor rate sits inside that band **[assumption]**.

### 2.2 But the floor is not the right target
Two corrections:

**(a) The real alternative is not the tutoring center.** A junior who aced organic chemistry is choosing between your platform, a research position, an internship, a lab job, or simply not working. The relevant comparison for a high-performing STEM student is a $15–25/hour campus technical job or an internship with resume value. **[assumption — reasoning, not data]**

**(b) Paid-per-session work has hidden unpaid time.** Travel to the meeting spot, reviewing the current homework set, re-learning material from three semesters ago, and coordinating scheduling. A $25/hour session rate with 30 minutes of prep is really ~$17/hour. This is exactly the complaint that dogs Varsity Tutors and BetterHelp contractors (v1 §1.5, §4).

### 2.3 My recommendation
**Target $25–30/hour take-home to the tutor, i.e. roughly 2x the campus wage floor.** Rationale:
- It is unambiguously better than any campus job, which makes recruiting a one-line pitch.
- It survives the prep-time haircut and still beats the alternatives.
- It is affordable: at a 20–25% take rate, the student pays ~$32–40/hour. That sits at or below the low end of the observed college tutoring market ($18–118/hour online; college-student tutors typically charge $35–75/hour) **[verified]**, so you are *cheaper than a private tutor* while *paying better than the university*. That is a defensible position on both sides of the marketplace simultaneously, and it is rare.
- Take rate at 20–25% also beats Wyzant's combined 34% (25% tutor + 9% student) and Preply's 33% entry rate **[verified, v1]** — say so out loud when recruiting.

**What actually drives reliable attendance, beyond the rate** — and this matters more than the number:
- **Density of bookings.** A tutor who gets 6 sessions a week in one course they already know shows up. A tutor who gets one session a month forgets you exist. This is the strongest argument for launching in very few courses (§9) — concentration is what makes the supply side worth a tutor's time.
- **Low prep burden.** Same course, same professor, repeatedly. The second session of CH 101 costs the tutor almost no prep. Course concentration is a supply-economics lever, not just a demand lever.
- **Predictable recurring slots**, so it lands in a class schedule like a job.
- **Fast payout.** Students are cash-constrained; weekly or per-session payout is a real differentiator.
- **Status.** "Verified CH 101 tutor" on a resume/LinkedIn is worth real money to a sophomore. Cheap for you to provide.
- **Reliability enforcement on the student side.** A tutor who gets stood up twice quits. The student-side reliability score the founder already specified is, in my view, primarily a *supply retention* mechanism — pitch it to tutors that way.

**Cancellation policy is a supply-side issue, not a policy detail.** If a student no-shows, the tutor must still be paid something. Otherwise the reliability risk is borne entirely by the party with the least money and the most alternatives. **Flag to ARCHITECTURE and UX: late-cancel and no-show handling with partial tutor payment needs to exist in v1.**

## 3. Competing with free — the load-bearing assumption

### 3.1 What free actually looks like at UA [verified]
The **Center for Academic Success** provides tutoring by trained peer tutors in "a variety of math, science and business classes," **walk-in, no appointment**, plus **Supplemental Instruction for many quantitative courses**, plus study-skills workshops. The **Capstone Center for Student Success** (Russell Hall) bundles these and **all services are free to registered UA students.** This is a well-resourced flagship. Free is genuinely good here — do not pitch against its quality.

### 3.2 The number that makes the business plausible [verified]
Tyton Partners, September 2024 survey:
- **Only 34% of students are even aware** their institution provides tutoring and academic support.
- **Only 13% engage with these services.** (Another framing: roughly one in eight students uses tutoring despite most institutions offering it.)
- Under 2% of students with disabilities and online-only learners use them.

**So free tutoring has ~87% non-consumption.** Meanwhile gateway-course DFW rates are enormous: introductory chemistry averaged **29.4% DFWI across 31 institutions** (Gardner Institute); Montana State reported **35% DFW in College Chemistry I and 47% in Calculus I**; College Algebra, Precalculus, Calculus I and Intro Chemistry all average **>25%** D/F/withdraw. DFWI rates for Black and Latinx students in intro chemistry across those 31 institutions were **above 40%**. **[all verified]**

Huge unmet need, tiny consumption of the free solution. That gap is the business.

### 3.3 Why students pay anyway — ranked by how much I believe each

I find these genuinely convincing:
1. **Free doesn't scale to the peak.** Demand is violently concentrated in the 72 hours before an exam, and a walk-in center has fixed staffing. Exactly when the student needs help most, free is a queue. **A guaranteed booked slot at 9pm two nights before the exam is a real product that free structurally cannot offer.** This is my single strongest argument and it should be tested first.
2. **Course-and-professor specificity.** A trained generalist peer tutor in "math" is not the same as someone who took *this section* with *this professor* and has seen how they write exams. This is the founder's wedge and it is the right one — it is precisely what free centers, staffed by subject rather than by section, don't provide.
3. **Walk-in is the wrong modality for anxious students.** Showing up at a public room and admitting you're lost in front of peers is a social cost. A private, pre-booked 1:1 with someone your own age removes it. (Note the founder's rejection of a public student ability rating is directionally consistent with this — good instinct.)
4. **Coverage gaps.** Free covers "a variety of" math/science/business classes. It does not cover everything, and SI is limited to "many quantitative" courses. **There will be high-enrollment, high-DFW courses with thin or no free coverage. Find them — that is the launch list (§9).**
5. **Scheduling.** Center hours vs. a student's actual free time, especially nights and weekends.

I find these weak, and the founder should not lean on them:
- "Better quality than the center." Unprovable at launch, and CAS tutors are trained. Don't claim it.
- "Students value what they pay for." Folk wisdom, not evidence.

### 3.4 The honest counter-argument
**The 13% figure cuts both ways, and this is the part to be uncomfortable about.** It may mean latent demand. It may also mean most students simply do not seek help *at any price* — that the binding constraint is help-seeking behavior, not access or cost. If that's true, a paid product cannot exceed free's penetration; it competes for a slice of that same 13%, minus everyone who's price-sensitive.

**The resolution: your initial market is not the non-consumers. It is the students who are already paying.** Students currently using Wyzant/Varsity Tutors, hiring a private tutor via word of mouth, buying Chegg-style subscriptions, or paying an upperclassman in cash. That population has demonstrated willingness to pay; you are offering a better, cheaper, more course-specific version. Converting non-help-seekers is upside, not the plan.

**This must be validated before anything is built.** The cheapest possible test, and the founder should run it this month: stand outside the CAS/Learning Commons and the relevant lecture halls in the week before a midterm, and ask students in the target courses two questions — *have you ever paid for tutoring in this course, and what did you pay?* **If fewer than ~1 in 10 have ever paid, the thesis is in trouble and nothing should be built yet.** That threshold is my judgment, not a benchmark **[assumption]**.

## 4. Willingness to pay → package price

### 4.1 Reference points [verified]
- Online college-level tutoring: **$18–118/hour**; "college tutoring rates generally start at $60–70/hour."
- **College students acting as tutors typically charge $35–75/hour.**
- General private tutoring: **$25–80/hour**; STEM runs 15–30% above humanities.
- Wyzant average: ~**$60/hour** (v1).
- **[unverified]** I found no direct survey of college students' *willingness to pay* — published data is about what tutors charge, not what students will bear. Treat every number below as a reasoned proposal to be tested, not a finding.

### 4.2 Proposal
Price the session at **$30–40**, with the tutor taking **$25–30**.

Why this band:
- It undercuts every commercial alternative while beating every campus wage.
- It is legible against things students already buy. A session should cost about what a night out costs — comparable to a couple of dinners, not to a textbook.
- Below ~$25 the tutor economics collapse against the $17.50 athletics midpoint plus prep time. Above ~$45 you are in Wyzant territory, competing on a dimension (brand, selection) where you lose.

**Package shape.** The founder's "MATH 125 — through the final" is right in spirit, but a full-semester upfront ask collides with the stated cash constraint. My recommendation: **anchor packages to exams, not to the semester.**

A typical gateway course has 3–4 exams plus a final **[assumption — verify against actual UA syllabi]**. So sell an **exam block**: 4 sessions leading to the next exam, ~$120–160. That is:
- A believable ask for a broke sophomore.
- Aligned with when students actually decide to get help — the moment of panic is after a bad exam grade, not in week one.
- Naturally repeating. A student who does well on Exam 2 buys the Exam 3 block. Renewal at the exam boundary is a far better retention mechanic than a semester-long commitment, because it gives you 3–4 shots at proving value instead of one.
- Compatible with an optional "through the final" bundle at a discount for those who want it, which is exactly the founder's framing and should be offered as the *upsell*, not the *default*.

**First session:** the founder's call — a paid first session under a self-serve refund guarantee — is correct and I want to endorse it explicitly. A free first session is the Preply trial-lesson problem inverted; whoever eats it, someone resents it. Paid-with-refund filters for intent, pays the tutor, and still de-risks the buyer.

**Note for the high-dosage evidence from v1:** that literature (~0.29–0.37 SD; high-dosage 15–20x low-dosage; same tutor over months) is K-12 and mostly school-day-embedded. **I am explicitly down-weighting it here.** It still supports *tutor continuity within a course*, which the package structure preserves. It does not support demanding 3 sessions/week from a college student, and I would not build the product around that claim.

## 5. Seasonality: what a revenue year looks like

### 5.1 The shape
A UA academic year is roughly: Fall mid-August to early December, Spring early January to early May, then a long summer. **[General US flagship calendar; verify exact UA dates and exam weeks with the contact.]**

Within each term the demand curve is not flat — it has teeth:
- **Weeks 1–3:** near zero. Nobody thinks they need help.
- **First exam (~week 5–6):** the first real spike, and the single most important acquisition moment of the semester. **A bad first exam grade is the conversion event for this entire business.**
- **Trough, then successive exam spikes**, each larger than the last as the stakes compound.
- **Finals (last 1–2 weeks):** the maximum spike, but also the lowest-value sessions — cramming, one-offs, no relationship.
- **Between terms and all summer:** near zero.

So: **two terms, roughly 4–5 demand spikes per year, and about 8 productive months.** My estimate is that a majority of annual revenue lands in ~8–10 weeks of exam runup. **[assumption — a structural judgment, not measured. Worth instrumenting from day one.]**

### 5.2 Surviving June–August
1. **Plan for it in the cost structure.** Peer tutors are variable cost, which is the saving grace — you are not carrying salaried supply through a dead quarter. Keep fixed costs near zero so summer is quiet, not fatal. This is a strong argument against hiring before campus #2.
2. **Summer sessions are real, and better than they look.** Compressed 4–8 week terms cram a full course into a fraction of the time, which *increases* tutoring need per enrolled student. Volume is a fraction of fall, but intensity per student is higher. **[assumption on UA summer enrollment volume — verify.]**
3. **Summer is the build-and-recruit quarter, and that is its actual job.** Recruit and verify tutors for fall (students who *just* finished the course in spring are the freshest and most available supply, and they're bored in June). Lock course selection against fall registration data. Prepare campus #2.
4. **Own the first-exam moment in late September.** The whole fall depends on being present and known in weeks 4–6. Summer is when you buy that.
5. **[Not recommended at launch, but note it]** incoming-freshman placement/prep in July–August is an adjacent product with summer demand. Don't build it; know it exists.
6. **Do not paper over the trough with a subscription.** The founder already rejected time-subscriptions; seasonality is a second independent reason that was right. A student who is billed monthly through a dead month churns and does not come back.

**Direct consequence for planning:** judge the business on a **fall-to-spring cycle**, not on months. One full cycle is the minimum unit of evidence, and that means a real answer takes about nine months.

## 6. Structural supply churn

### 6.1 The problem, stated precisely
Undergraduate tutors have a maximum ~3-year useful life (you can't recruit them until they've taken the course, so realistically they're sophomores through seniors), and **the best ones leave fastest** — they get research positions, internships, REUs, and they graduate on time. Meanwhile the supply you most want is the *most recent* cohort, who took the course under the *current* professor. There is an inherent tension: the freshest knowledge belongs to the least experienced tutors.

This is Yik Yak's structural weakness ("fell apart when they left for summer or graduated") applied to the supply side, and it is permanent. It cannot be solved, only managed.

### 6.2 Mitigations, best first

1. **Make the course, not the tutor, the durable asset. — the key move, and it is an architecture decision.** Every session should deposit something reusable into a per-course knowledge base: what topics students struggle with, how this professor structures exams, common errors, worked approaches, which practice materials help. When a tutor graduates, the *course* retains its accumulated knowledge and the replacement tutor onboards in an hour instead of a semester. This converts churn from an existential threat into an onboarding cost, and it is a genuine moat that Knack's booking-layer model does not obviously have. **Flag to ARCHITECTURE: a course-scoped knowledge/notes entity, populated from session artifacts, should exist in the v1 data model even if the v1 UI barely surfaces it.**
2. **Recruit deliberately by class year.** Prefer rising sophomores and juniors (2–3 years of runway) over seniors (months). Recruit each spring from the cohort that just finished the course.
3. **Add a stabilizing layer: graduate students and former course TAs.** Longer tenure (often 4–6 years), deeper subject knowledge, and frequently *more* familiarity with the professor than any undergraduate. They are also more expensive and less peer-like. A blend is right — undergrads for relatability and price, grad students for continuity and coverage of the hard courses.
4. **Alumni remote tutors.** A tutor who graduates still knows the course and professor, and a course's structure typically persists for years. Remote sessions extend their useful life. Weaker on rapport; free to try.
5. **Treat recruiting as a permanent, seasonal function, not a launch task.** You must replace roughly a third of supply annually **[assumption]**. Build a spring recruiting cycle into the operating rhythm from year one.
6. **Referral from tutors.** Your best CH 101 tutor knows who else did well in CH 101. This is the cheapest supply channel that exists and it should be in v1.
7. **A hidden risk to watch: professor turnover.** Your entire wedge is "knows this professor's exams." When a course changes instructor, that course's tutor roster silently loses its edge. **Track instructor-by-section as a first-class field and treat a professor change as a re-qualification event.** Nobody will think of this later. **Flag to ARCHITECTURE.**

Khanmigo's ~15% regular-usage rate among students with access **[verified, v1]** is the evidence that AI has an adoption problem humans don't. Do not compete on explanation quality. |

### Is there a reason nobody has won this?
Yes, and I'd name three:
1. **The unit of liquidity is tiny and perishable.** Not "a campus" but "this course, this section, this week." It evaporates every semester and every graduation.
2. **Free is right there, and it is decent.** Every campus has a tutoring center, so the paid product must justify itself against $0 (§3).
3. **It doesn't compound.** Each campus is a fresh cold start (§1.3), so the growth curve is linear in launch labor. That is unattractive to venture capital, which is likely *why* Knack moved to institutional contracts — B2B contracts compound in a way D2C campus-by-campus does not.

**Point 3 is the strategic crux.** If the founder wants a venture-scale outcome, the campus-by-campus D2C path probably converts to institutional sales eventually, exactly as Knack's did. If the founder wants a real, profitable, self-funded business at a handful of campuses, D2C is a fine terminal state. **These are different companies and the founder should say which one they are building.** It is now my #1 open question.

## 8. Revised recommendation: does "Term Engagement" survive?

**Yes, and the founder's constraints improved it.** Course packages are a better instantiation of the idea than my v1 term subscription. The core bet was always *committed cadence + continuity + a defined end*, and "MATH 125 through the final" delivers all three more legibly than "12 weeks × 2/week."

Mapping v1 → v2:

| v1 mechanic | v2 form | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Curated shortlist of 3 | Card deck of **all** tutors for the course (~5–20), double opt-in | **Better.** Course scoping makes the catalog naturally small, so "show everything" *is* curation. Double opt-in also protects supply from bad matches — a genuine improvement I did not propose. |
| Per-subject term engagement | **Per-course package, exam-anchored** (§4.2) | **Better.** Tighter scope, clearer end, smaller ask. |
| Free first session | **Paid first session + self-serve refund** | **Better.** Avoids the Preply trial-clawback resentment; tutor is paid either way. |
| Parent progress reports as retention engine | **Dead — no parent.** Replaced by: **the exam grade is the report.** | Changed, and this is the biggest structural shift. Retention now runs on the student's own result at the next exam. Renewal should be *asked for right after the exam grade posts*, which is a concrete, dateable product moment. |
| High-dosage K-12 evidence as justification | Down-weighted (§4.2) | Keep continuity-within-course; drop the dosage prescription. |
| Minors: consent, background checks, recording, three UIs | **All gone** | Enormous simplification. See §10. |
| Per-subject plurality (student needs maths *and* chem) | Native — packages are per course by construction | Preserved automatically. |

**One thing I want to push back on, gently.** "Through the final" as the *default* package risks a large upfront ask that the founder has already identified as a problem. My recommendation is that the exam-block package is the default and "through the final" is a discounted upsell. Same product, better first-purchase conversion, and 3–4 renewal moments per semester instead of one. This is a pricing-page decision, not an architecture one.

## 9. Course selection framework

Do not guess. Score candidate courses on six factors — the first three are the screen, the last three are the tiebreak.

**Screening factors (a course must pass all three):**
1. **Enrollment volume.** Raw addressable students per term. UA has ~35,600 undergraduates **[verified, fall 2025]**, so gateway sequences will have hundreds to low thousands per term. Below ~200/term, liquidity is too thin.
2. **DFW rate.** The pain signal. Target courses in the >25% band that the national data flags: College Algebra, Precalculus, Calculus I, Intro Chemistry, and by extension organic chemistry, intro accounting, intro economics, intro physics **[verified as national patterns; UA-specific rates must come from the contact — UA institutional research or the registrar may publish these, or the department will know]**.
3. **Tutor availability.** Are there enough students who recently took it and did well, who are still enrolled? A course taken by 800 students/term with a 30% DFW rate still leaves ~500 who passed — supply is not the constraint at this volume, *finding and vetting* them is.

**Tiebreak factors:**
4. **Free-coverage gap.** CAS covers "a variety of math, science and business classes" and SI covers "many quantitative" courses **[verified]** — which means some high-DFW courses are covered thinly or not at all, and some are covered but capacity-saturated at peak. **Concrete task for the UA contact: get the actual CAS tutoring subject list, the SI course list, and the center's hours. The best launch course is high-volume, high-DFW, and thinly covered.**
5. **Exam structure.** Prefer courses with 3–4 discrete, high-stakes, dateable exams. These create natural package boundaries, predictable demand spikes, and an unambiguous outcome. Avoid courses graded mostly on continuous assessment or projects — no spike, no urgency, no clean package.
6. **Professor and curriculum stability.** A course taught by the same 1–2 professors for years compounds your knowledge asset (§6.2). A course with 12 rotating instructors fragments your tutor pool into tiny per-section slices and destroys the wedge.

**Practical advice on scope: launch with 2–3 courses, not 10.** Concentration is what creates both demand density (everyone in the course hears about you) and supply density (a tutor gets enough bookings to care, §2.3). Breadth is the enemy of liquidity in the first semester. Add courses only when the existing ones have tutors with idle capacity.

**Sequencing note:** prefer a **fall-heavy** course for launch (intro courses skew fall), and launch in time for the **first exam in weeks 5–6**, not week 1.

## 10. Flags for ARCHITECTURE and UX

**Huge simplifications from dropping minors** — both teammates should delete this work from v1: no parent↔student identity graph, no versioned guardianship consent, no background-check pipeline, no mandatory session recording/retention, no third logged-in persona, no COPPA. Payer = user = one account. This removes what I previously called the dominant compliance surface.

**New requirements this round creates:**

**ARCHITECTURE:**
1. **Course/section/professor is the central entity**, and it is term-scoped. Sourcing UA's catalog (scrape, manual seed, or ask) is a real, unglamorous v1 task — and it is also the thing that resets at campus #2. Design for multi-campus namespacing from the start even though v1 is one campus; that's cheap now and expensive later.
2. **Professor is a first-class field on a section, and a professor change is a tutor re-qualification event** (§6.2). Nobody will remember this later.
3. **The semester is a hard time boundary.** Packages expire. Tutor qualifications are relative to when they took the course. Data ages out. Build the calendar in.
4. **Double opt-in is a request/accept state machine** with expiry, decline reasons, and a fallback path when no tutor accepts. The "nobody accepted" case is the one that silently kills trust — design it explicitly.
5. **Hidden quality scoring needs event logging from day one**, well before the Bayesian/bandit layer exists. Log session completion, renewal, no-show, cancel, and any post-session signal. You cannot backfill this. Never expose a number.
6. **No-show / late-cancel handling with partial tutor payment** must be in v1 (§2.3). It is a supply-retention feature, not an edge case.
7. **Per-course knowledge asset** populated from session artifacts (§6.2) — the durable moat against tutor churn.
8. **.edu email verification** is the campus gate and the cheapest anti-fraud and community-boundary mechanism available. It replaces the whole identity apparatus minors would have required.
9. **Self-serve refund flow** with abuse limits (the guarantee is a product promise, so it must be one click, but it also must not be farmable).
10. **Off-platform leakage is a live risk** (§7) — two adults on the same campus can trivially meet privately. The defense must be convenience, not policy.
11. **Summer dormancy:** the app must degrade to near-zero activity without looking abandoned. Empty states matter more here than in most products.

**UX:**
1. **The card deck is the product's defining screen, and with no stars, the hard question is: what goes on a card?** My recommendation: the grade they earned, the semester they took it, **the professor they took it with**, availability in the next 7 days, price, and 1–2 lines in their own words. Course-specific evidence replaces social proof. Get this screen right and the rest follows.
2. **The conversion moment is right after a bad exam grade.** The product should be findable, bookable, and comprehensible by a panicking student at 11pm. Time-to-first-booking is the metric.
3. **Renewal should be prompted at the exam boundary**, not on a billing date.
4. **Price visible before signup** (carried forward from v1 — the anti-BetterHelp principle still holds).
5. **The student-side reliability score must never be a visible number**, per the founder's decision — but the *behaviors* it measures should be nudged visibly (confirmations, reminders, easy reschedule). Design the nudges, hide the score.
6. **Distribution is physical.** Someone will need shareable artifacts — a QR code for a flyer outside a lecture hall, a link that pastes cleanly into a GroupMe. Design for those two surfaces specifically.
7. Academic integrity: per the founder's decision, this is a ToS line plus one sentence in tutor onboarding. **My only addition — and it's about distribution, not compliance:** being visibly clean about past exams and current assignments is what keeps professors and advisors willing to refer students to you. NoteShark named faculty relations as its cause of death **[verified]**. That's the reason to bother, and it's the founder's stated reason too.
